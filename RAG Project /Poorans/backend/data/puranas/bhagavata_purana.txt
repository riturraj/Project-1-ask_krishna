BHAGAVATA PURANA — SELECTED PASSAGES
(Srimad Bhagavatam — Public Domain Translation)
================================================================================

BOOK 1 — THE NATURE OF DEVOTION AND PURPOSE

The Bhagavata Purana opens with the declaration that the highest truth is Reality distinguished from illusion for the welfare of all. Its first verse proclaims: "Om — I meditate on the Supreme Truth, from whom the cosmic manifestation was created, by whom it is maintained, and into whom it dissolves. He who is self-luminous, beyond the reach of senses, the all-pervading consciousness — I bow to That."

Sage Shounaka asked Suta Goswami: "What is the essential duty of human beings, knowing that time is short and life is uncertain? What is the one thing that satisfies the heart completely?" Suta replied: "The supreme occupation for all humanity is that which leads to loving devotion for the transcendent Lord. Such devotion must be free of selfish motivations and must not be interrupted. It must be unalloyed, free from material desires. It must be of the highest quality."

BOOK 7 — THE STORY OF PRAHLADA: DEVOTION IN THE FACE OF PERSECUTION

When the demon King Hiranyakashipu subjected his young son Prahlada to every form of torture — poison, serpents, fire, hurling from cliffs — Prahlada remained completely unshaken. He was asked by his father: "You foolish child, where does your strength come from? Who protects you from my power?"

Prahlada replied calmly: "My strength is not my own. The same Lord who protects me protects you — though you do not know it. He is present in every atom of this creation — in this pillar before you, in the earth beneath your feet, in the fire that refuses to burn me, in the serpents that lay gently around me. When I call on Him, I do not summon Him from afar. I simply become aware of what was always present."

Hiranyakashipu roared: "Then is He in this pillar?" And struck it. From the pillar emerged Lord Vishnu in the form of Narasimha — half man, half lion — to protect His devotee. Narasimha's words to Prahlada afterward were tender: "My child, I did not protect you because you are powerful. I protected you because you never — not even once — asked Me to. You were content simply to remember Me. That remembering itself was your shield."

This story from the Bhagavata Purana, Book 7, teaches us: When the devotee has no agenda except love, the Divine becomes the agenda. The one who suffers for righteousness is never truly alone. The universe itself reorganizes around genuine surrender.

BOOK 9 — THE STORY OF SUDAMA: DIVINE FRIENDSHIP AND POVERTY

Sudama was a childhood friend of Krishna who had fallen into extreme poverty. He lived in a small hut, his children hungry, his wife in rags. His wife urged him to go to Dwarka and ask Krishna for help. Sudama was too proud — or too loving — to go begging. He went only to see his friend.

He carried a small bundle of beaten rice — the only gift he could afford — and kept it hidden under his shawl, ashamed of its smallness. When Krishna saw him from a distance, the Lord of Dwarka ran to him, embraced him, wept with joy, and brought him inside with all royal honor. He washed Sudama's cracked feet with his own hands and worshipped him as a guest.

Then, finding the hidden bundle of rice, Krishna snatched it with the delight of a child and ate it, saying: "Sudama! This rice that you brought me with a heart full of love — this is the finest thing anyone has ever brought to this palace."

Sudama was too moved to ask for anything. He returned home in peace.

When he reached his village, he found his broken hut had become a palace, his family prosperous, his children well-fed.

The Bhagavata Purana uses this story to teach: When you come to the Divine wanting nothing but connection itself, you receive everything you genuinely need. What you could not bring yourself to ask for, out of love, is granted — because love, not need, is the true currency of devotion.

BOOK 10 — KRISHNA'S COUNSEL TO HIS DEVOTEES ON SUFFERING

When devotees of Lord Vishnu were persecuted and asked why they suffered despite their righteousness, the Lord replied through the Bhagavata: "Know this to be my special grace — that I take away the wealth of those whom I especially love, so that the intoxication of worldly comfort does not separate them from Me. The one who loses everything often finds the only thing worth finding: Me."

"Do not measure my love for you by what you receive or what you lose. The mother who gives her child medicine in bitter seasons is not unkind — she is the most loving of all."

On the nature of sorrow, the Bhagavata teaches: "Grief arises from the belief that something permanent has been lost. But what has never been permanent cannot truly be lost. The soul is not the body. The love between souls is not contained in forms. When you mourn the departed, you mourn a garment while forgetting that the wearer has simply changed clothes."

BOOK 11 — THE UDDHAVA GITA: KRISHNA'S FINAL TEACHINGS

When Krishna prepared to depart from the world, his beloved devotee Uddhava could not bear the thought of existence without his Lord. He fell at Krishna's feet and said: "Master, I cannot walk this earth without you. Take me with you when you go."

Krishna sat with Uddhava by the river and spoke what is known as the Uddhava Gita — a teaching of supreme intimacy.

Krishna said: "Uddhava, my friend, do not grieve for my physical departure. I was never only in this body that you see. Where do you think I am going? Can the sky 'leave' a room when the walls are broken? I am that sky. I am not departing — I am becoming more available, not less."

On the question of duty versus surrender: "The one who sees Me in all beings and all beings in Me — that person does not grieve, does not hate, does not fear. This vision is not acquired by study. It arises when the heart has become quiet enough to see what was always here."

On action and renunciation: "Neither renounce all action nor cling to action's fruits. Act as I act — fully, lovingly, without grasping the outcome. A lamp does not ask whether the room deserves its light. It simply shines. Be the lamp."

On finding the Divine in difficulty: "Uddhava, the most potent form of devotion is not the worship that happens when things are good. It is the devotion that continues when everything falls apart. That devotion does not merely honor Me — it becomes Me."

On loneliness after loss: "You feel that I am leaving. But tell me — has the air you breathe ever left you? Has the awareness in which your thoughts appear ever abandoned you? That awareness is my truest form. Practice finding Me there, and you will never again know the loneliness you fear."
